Real estate contract disputes in Queens

Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Updated November 8, 2026

When a real estate closing falls through, the contract, the closing notices, and what each side was prepared to do often determine who defaulted and what happens to the deposit. We represent buyers and sellers in Queens real estate contract disputes.

What happens when a buyer or seller misses the closing?

Start with the signed contract and riders, then put any extensions, closing notices, and objections in date order. A New York sale contract with an “on or about” closing date ordinarily allows a reasonable time to perform unless the parties made time of the essence. The controlling deadline can come from the contract itself or from an effective later notice. Kugel v Reynolds, 228 AD3d 743 (2d Dept 2024).

For a buyer, evidence may include proof of available funds or financing and timely objections about title or unmet closing conditions. For a seller, it may include the deed and other closing documents, proof that required title issues could be resolved, and a record of what happened on the scheduled date. If title is disputed, the contract can determine which issues must be cleared and when. Treasure Is. of Asbury Park Self-Storage, LLC v MBAR Realty, LLC, 216 AD3d 1200 (2d Dept 2023).

What makes a time-of-the-essence notice effective?

A notice setting a firm closing should clearly state that time is of the essence, fix a closing date that gives the other party reasonable time to perform, and warn that missing the deadline will be treated as a default. What counts as reasonable depends on the circumstances. Check the contract’s delivery requirements and preserve proof of how and when the notice was sent. ADC Orange, Inc. v Coyote Acres, Inc., 7 NY3d 484 (2006) and Kugel v Reynolds, 228 AD3d 743 (2d Dept 2024).

Who gets the down payment?

A seller may be entitled to keep the down payment if the buyer defaults without a lawful excuse. A liquidated-damages clause may make the deposit the seller’s sole remedy. The outcome can differ if the seller is in default or the buyer has a valid right to cancel. Read the default, cancellation, and escrow provisions together to determine what each side can claim and how the funds may be released. Ashkenazi v Miller, 190 AD3d 668 (2d Dept 2021).

If financing falls through, look at the exact mortgage contingency. Check what it covers, what proof the buyer must provide, and how and when the buyer must give notice to cancel. A lender’s denial and a failure to fund after issuing a commitment can raise different questions under the rider. Carpio v Morris, 223 AD3d 781 (2d Dept 2024).

Can a buyer force the seller to close?

Specific performance is a court order requiring a party to perform the sale contract. A buyer seeking that remedy generally must show that it substantially performed its obligations, was ready, willing, and able to close when required, and that the seller defaulted and was able to convey the property. The buyer must also show that money damages would be inadequate. The contract may limit the available remedies. Treasure Is. of Asbury Park Self-Storage, LLC v MBAR Realty, LLC, 216 AD3d 1200 (2d Dept 2023).

A missed closing and a disputed deposit

Our firm represented the seller in 1211 Gilbert Ave. Holdings LLC v Berri. The Bronx County court upheld the time-of-the-essence notice, dismissed the buyer’s claims, and allowed the seller to retain the $35,000 deposit and escrow interest. The court also canceled the notice of pendency. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome. Read the Berri case note.

What documents should you gather?

  • The signed contract of sale, all riders and amendments, and any written extensions.
  • Closing notices, default or cancellation letters, and proof of delivery.
  • Emails and texts about scheduling, adjournments, and objections.
  • The deposit receipt, escrow instructions, and any demand for release of the funds.
  • The title report and responses to objections, plus loan commitments, denials, or proof of funds, as applicable.
  • Closing documents and any record of who appeared or was prepared to close.
