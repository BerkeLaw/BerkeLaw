Access to a neighbor’s property for construction in Queens

Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Updated November 8, 2026

Work on a Queens property sometimes requires scaffolding over a neighbor’s roof, an inspection from an adjoining yard, or temporary protections on the next building. A written access agreement can set the ground rules. When access is necessary and permission has been refused, New York’s RPAPL § 881 provides a way to ask the court for a license.

Request a Construction Access Consultation

When can an owner seek access under RPAPL § 881?

An owner or lessee can seek a license when improvements or repairs cannot be made in a commercially reasonable manner without entering the adjoining premises and permission has been refused. The statute identifies uses such as documenting preconstruction conditions, installing and removing scaffolding or protective coverings, monitoring cracks or vibration, and temporary projections into neighboring airspace. The particular work and property determine what access is justified. The statute excludes court-ordered licenses for adjoining property owned, leased, or occupied by a state entity as defined in the law. RPAPL § 881(1)–(3), (6).

What should an access request include?

Explain the work and why it requires entry. A useful request identifies the people who need access, the portions of the adjoining property affected, the proposed work hours, and the dates each protection is expected to go up and come down. Include the available plans, engineering reports, permits, and insurance information. Explain which alternatives were considered and how the project will protect entrances, yards, parking, tenants, or business operations.

If you received the request, respond in writing. Ask for the plans and schedule, identify any affected occupants, and flag particular concerns such as a blocked driveway, loss of roof use, or existing damage. Keep the request, envelopes, delivery records, and responses. Under the current statute, more than one written notice served by certified mail on the owner with no response within 60 days can qualify as refusal. Whether an exchange amounts to refusal depends on its facts. In a 2026 case, the court found that requests for documentation and an inspection did not establish refusal on that record. RPAPL § 881(1)(d) and Matter of Board of Mgrs. of the Claremont S. Condominium v Hudson Mews Apt. Corp., 2026 NY Slip Op 31858(U).

What should a construction access license address?

  • Scope and schedule. Attach plans showing where people and equipment may go. Set dates, hours, advance notice, site contacts, and a procedure for extensions.
  • Protection and monitoring. Document existing conditions with dated photos or a survey. Specify temporary protection, who will inspect it, and how the parties will respond to movement, leaks, or a safety issue.
  • Insurance and responsibility. Identify everyone who will enter and review the applicable commercial general liability coverage. If the agreement calls for additional insured protection, confirm that the endorsement reflects what the parties agreed.
  • Removal and restoration. Set deadlines for taking down protections and restoring affected areas. Specify how to report damage, who will make repairs, and how the parties will document completion.
  • Compensation and review costs. Address any loss of use, the amount and timing of payment, and how to handle reasonable fees for reviewing the plans.

What if the parties cannot agree?

An applicant may bring a special proceeding under CPLR Article 4. The petition must explain why entry is necessary and the dates sought. The court can set terms based on the requested access and the burden on the neighbor. A court-issued license carries conditions for reasonable advance notice, the duration of access, and documents for covered work. The licensee must make commercially reasonable efforts to adhere to the projected schedule and seek an extension from the court when it cannot do so. Insurance documentation must be sufficient to allow the adjoining owner or identified lessees, as applicable, to make a third-party claim for damage or injury caused by the entry. RPAPL § 881(2), (4).

The statute requires reasonable compensation for loss of use and enjoyment, including diminution in value. The licensee is also liable for actual damages caused by the entry. A court can order reimbursement of reasonable fees for reviewing relevant documents. What amounts are appropriate depends on the record and the scope of the work. RPAPL § 881(2), (4)(e), (5)(b).

What should you bring to a consultation?

Gather the proposed license, plans and surveys, notices and delivery records, construction schedule, and insurance documents. Bring photographs, any prior access agreement, and a list of the areas or occupants that the work would affect. If work is scheduled or an access notice has arrived, include the date when requesting a consultation.
