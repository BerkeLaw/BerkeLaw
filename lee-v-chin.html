Lee v Chin (App Div, 1st Dept 2026): lease riders, a merger clause, and a dismissed fraud claim

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In Lee v Chin, 2026 NY Slip Op 01851, decided March 26, 2026, the New York Appellate Division, First Department unanimously reversed an order of Supreme Court, New York County, and granted the defendant’s motion to dismiss two claims: a fraudulent inducement claim and a declaratory judgment claim asking the court to treat a lease as ending in April 2025 rather than April 2024. The court pointed to the plaintiff’s own review of draft riders that “plainly stated an April 2024 end date” and to the lease’s merger clause. The decision addresses how those two claims were pleaded on a motion to dismiss; it is not a ruling on every issue between the parties.

What was the dispute about?

As described in the decision, the plaintiff alleged that the defendant, through his agents, represented that the parties’ lease would reflect an agreed-upon 54-month rent structure; that the parties met and agreed on those terms; that the defendant’s agents exchanged the lease without drawing the plaintiff’s attention to a shorter extension period and termination date; and that the plaintiff relied on the representation that the final lease embodied the agreed terms. The plaintiff relied primarily on a September 12, 2020 email in which, in the court’s words, the parties “seem to have agreed that the lease would expire in April 2025.” The signed lease and rider stated an April 2024 end date.

What did the court decide about the fraudulent inducement claim?

The First Department held that the plaintiff “failed to adequately plead a cause of action for fraudulent inducement.” Two points drove that conclusion:

  • A later email, dated September 18, 2020, indicated that the plaintiff “reviewed and proposed revisions to draft riders which plainly stated an April 2024 end date.”
  • The final lease and rider refuted the plaintiff’s allegations of any facts that were “unknown to one party but known to the other (who has misled the first),” the standard the court quoted from Chimart Assoc. v Paul, 66 NY2d 570, 573 (1986).

What did the court decide about the lease term and the merger clause?

The declaratory judgment claim sought a declaration that the lease was void insofar as it purported to terminate in April 2024 instead of April 2025. The court held that the plaintiff failed to state a cause of action: “The lease’s merger clause expressly barred any prior agreements. Absent any evidence of fraud, and in light of the merger clause,” there was no cause of action for a declaration that the lease would expire in April 2025 rather than April 2024 (citing New York First Ave. CVS v Wellington Tower Assoc., 299 AD2d 205, 206 [1st Dept 2002]). The court added that it had considered the plaintiff’s remaining arguments and found them unavailing.

What did the decision not decide?

  • Only two claims. The appeal concerned the order “to the extent appealed from,” which was the denial of dismissal of the second and third causes of action. The decision does not discuss any other claim in the case.
  • Pleading stage. The ruling came on a motion to dismiss and turned on the complaint and the documents in the record (the emails, draft riders, final lease, and rider). It is not a trial verdict or a finding about anyone’s honesty.
  • No blanket rule about merger clauses. The court tied its declaratory judgment ruling to the merger clause and the absence of any evidence of fraud. The decision does not say a merger clause defeats every fraud claim.
  • Fact-specific. The outcome depended on these documents. A case with different emails, drafts, or lease language can come out differently.

What should parties check before signing lease riders?

The decision is a reminder that the signed lease and riders, not earlier emails, usually control. Whether you are the landlord or the tenant, before signing:

Lease and rider pre-signing checklist
CheckWhy it matters
Read every rider as closely as the main lease form.Riders often hold the dates that matter most: commencement, expiration, extension, and renewal terms.
Compare the term and end date in the lease and riders against the emails, term sheet, or letter of intent.In Lee v Chin, the court relied on a later email showing the draft riders with the April 2024 end date had been reviewed.
Confirm the rent schedule matches the stated term.A rent schedule that runs longer or shorter than the stated term is a signal to stop and reconcile the documents before signing.
Find the merger (integration) clause.Expect earlier emails, conversations, and side understandings to carry little or no weight if they are not in the signed documents.
Put any negotiated term that matters into the signed lease or a signed amendment.An email confirming a deal point may not survive a merger clause.
Keep a record of the drafts you reviewed and your markups.Your own comments on drafts can later be used to show what you knew when you signed.
Have counsel review the final execution version, not just earlier drafts.Changes between drafts are where mismatches tend to appear.
