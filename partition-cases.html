Partition actions and inherited property in Queens

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In New York, a co-owner who holds and possesses real property as a joint tenant or tenant in common may bring an action to partition it: the court can divide the property or, if it cannot be divided without great prejudice to the owners, order a sale (RPAPL §§ 901, 915). An heir who inherited a share as a co-owner may sue whether or not the heir is in possession (§ 901(3)). If the Queens property is “heirs property” (generally, a residence held as tenants in common, with family ties among the owners and no written agreement governing partition), RPAPL § 993 adds a mandatory settlement conference, a court-determined fair market value, a buyout right for co-owners who did not ask for a sale, and a preference for an open-market sale over an auction. Standing, any co-ownership agreement, title problems, and open estate issues can change the path, so the deed, the estate, and the family facts need review first.

Who can bring a partition action in New York?

RPAPL § 901 allows:

  • a person holding and in possession of real property as a joint tenant or tenant in common, with an estate of inheritance, for life, or for years (§ 901(1));
  • an heir who is a joint tenant or tenant in common because of inheriting from someone who died holding and in possession of the property, whether or not the heir is in possession, even where there is an apparent devise to someone else (the heir must establish that the devise is void) (§ 901(3));
  • the executor or administrator of an estate that owns an interest in common, if the Surrogate approves (§ 901(4)); and
  • in limited cases, a holder of a future estate or reversion, subject to the present estate holder’s interest and written consent to any sale (§ 901(2)).

Who has to be named in the case?

RPAPL § 903 requires joining every person with an undivided share (in possession or not), every person entitled to a reversion, remainder, or inheritance of an undivided share, every person who may become entitled to a beneficial interest in an undivided share, and certain dower interests. An executor or administrator must be joined where letters have been issued on the estate from which the plaintiff’s title derives and the action is brought within eighteen months after the letters issued, or where the decedent would have been a party if living. If no executor or administrator has been appointed, the complaint must say so. Missing a necessary party is a common source of delay, so a current title search matters.

Will the court divide the property or order a sale?

Under RPAPL § 915, the interlocutory judgment determines each party’s share. If the property can be partitioned without great prejudice to the owners, the court directs a physical division and designates three disinterested freeholders as commissioners to make it. If partition cannot be made without great prejudice, the general statute directs a sale at public auction. Many Queens residences, such as a single house or a building that cannot practically be split into separate lots, raise this “great prejudice” question. Heirs property follows different rules (below).

What is “heirs property” under RPAPL § 993?

Under § 993(2)(e), heirs property is real property held in tenancy in common that meets all of these conditions when the partition action is filed:

  1. there is no agreement in a record binding all co-tenants that governs partition of the property;
  2. any co-tenant acquired title from a relative, living or deceased;
  3. the property is used for residential or agricultural purposes; and
  4. at least one of these applies: 20% or more of the interests are held by co-tenants who are relatives; 20% or more of the interests are held by someone who acquired title from a relative; 20% or more of the co-tenants are relatives of each other; or any co-tenant who acquired title from a relative lives in the property.

In any partition action, the court must decide, after notice and an opportunity to be heard, whether the property is heirs property. If it is, § 993 governs unless all co-tenants agree otherwise in a record (§ 993(3)(b)). The section was enacted in 2019 and applies to partition actions filed on or after its effective date (§ 993(3)(a)).

How does an heirs-property partition case work?

  1. Settlement conference. The plaintiff must file proof of service within 20 days of service, and the court must hold a mandatory settlement conference within 60 days after the request for judicial intervention is filed (or an agreed adjourned date). Both sides must negotiate in good faith toward options such as a tenancy-in-common agreement, a co-tenant buyout, partition in kind, or an open-market sale. If the court finds the plaintiff failed to negotiate in good faith, the action must be dismissed (§ 993(5)).
  2. Protection for defendants who have not answered. A defendant who appears at the settlement conference but did not timely answer is presumed to have a reasonable excuse and may answer within 30 days of that first appearance, with the default deemed vacated once the answer is served and filed (§ 993(5)(d)).
  3. Determination of value. Unless all co-tenants agree on a value or the court finds an appraisal not worth its cost, the court orders an appraisal by a disinterested licensed appraiser. Parties may object within 30 days after notice of the appraisal, and the court holds a hearing before determining fair market value (§ 993(6)).
  4. Buyout. A co-tenant who requests partition by sale agrees that the co-tenants who did not request a sale may buy that interest at the court-determined value. Eligible co-tenants have 45 days after notice of the value to elect, and the court then sets a payment date at least 60 days out (§ 993(7)).
  5. Partition in kind preferred. If the buyout does not resolve the case, the court must order partition in kind unless it finds that doing so would cause “great manifest prejudice” to the co-tenants as a group, weighing factors that include practicability of division, the effect on value, family history with the property, sentimental attachment, a resident co-tenant’s use, and each co-tenant’s contributions to taxes, insurance, and upkeep (§§ 993(8)–(9)).
  6. Sale, if ordered, is open-market first. If the court orders a sale, it is an open-market sale through a licensed broker at no less than the determined value, unless the court finds sealed bids or an auction more advantageous for the co-tenants as a group. If no co-tenant timely requested a sale and partition in kind is not ordered, the action is dismissed (§§ 993(8)(b), 993(10)).

Can someone who bought a family member’s share force a sale?

For heirs property, § 993(12) provides that no partition action related to heirs property may be initiated by a party who acquired a share “by means other than inheritance,” and who did not inherit it “directly from a person who was a co-tenant prior to the property becoming heirs property or from a co-tenant who was an heir thereto.” Section 993(13) also gives inheriting co-tenants and their relatives a right of first refusal to match a bona fide outside offer for a share, with priority to a co-tenant who lives in the property, and 180 days after notice to match. How courts apply these provisions in particular family and title situations is still developing, so they need careful review on the facts.

What about mortgage, taxes, repairs, and rent one owner collected?

Partition cases often include an accounting. The court may adjust the parties’ rights where one co-owner received more than a proper share of the rents or profits (RPAPL § 945). Co-owners also commonly seek credits for payments of mortgage, taxes, insurance, and necessary repairs or improvements, or argue offsets for one owner’s exclusive occupancy. Results depend on proof and equitable considerations, so records of who paid what, and when, are important. In heirs-property cases, each co-tenant’s contributions to taxes, insurance, and upkeep is also a statutory factor in deciding whether to divide the property (§ 993(9)(a)(vi)).

Does a written co-ownership agreement matter?

Yes. An “agreement in a record” (written or electronically stored) binding all co-tenants that governs partition takes the property out of the heirs-property definition (§ 993(2)(e)(i)), and the co-tenants can agree in a record to proceed differently (§ 993(3)(b)). Operating agreements, tenancy-in-common agreements, buy-sell terms, and settlement stipulations can also affect whether, when, and how a co-owner may seek partition. Read any agreement before filing or responding.

Where are partition cases about Queens property heard?

Partition actions concerning Queens real property are generally litigated in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, Queens County, at 88-11 Sutphin Boulevard, Jamaica. When the property is part of a decedent’s estate, the Surrogate’s Court may also be involved; for example, an executor or administrator needs the Surrogate’s approval to bring or join a partition action for the estate (RPAPL § 901(4)).

What documents should co-owners and heirs gather?

  • The current deed and prior deeds in the chain of title, plus a recent title search
  • Death certificates, wills, and any letters testamentary or letters of administration
  • Any co-ownership, tenancy-in-common, operating, or buy-sell agreement, and any prior settlement
  • Mortgage statements, real estate tax bills, water and sewer bills, and insurance policies, with proof of who paid
  • Receipts and contracts for repairs and improvements
  • Leases, rent records, and occupancy history (who lived there, and when)
  • Any appraisals, broker opinions, or offers received for the property or a share

Are there alternatives to litigation?

Often. Co-owners can negotiate a buyout, a voluntary sale with an agreed split of proceeds and credits, or a written co-ownership agreement. RPAPL § 993(5)(e) lists these kinds of resolutions as the goals of the heirs-property settlement conference. We represent co-owners and heirs in Queens partition and title disputes, whether the goal is a sale, a buyout, or keeping the property.
