Gardner v Lefkowitz (S.D.N.Y. 2019): default judgment vacated after Second Circuit remand

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In Gardner v. Lefkowitz, No. 1:05-cv-05193 (DAB) (JCF), a January 23, 2019 order of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Document 93), Judge Deborah A. Batts granted the defendant’s motion to vacate and vacated the default judgment “in light of the Second Circuit’s ruling.” The court denied a request to file a late reply and directed the defendant to move against or answer the complaint within 30 days, with no extensions.

That appellate ruling was a September 21, 2018 summary order in Piers Gardner v. Surie Lefkowitz, No. 17-1265 (2d Cir.). The court of appeals vacated the district court’s March 30, 2017 denial of a motion to vacate an October 2005 default judgment of $180,493.82 and remanded for further proceedings on the scope of the parties’ agreement. On May 1, 2019, the district court so-ordered a stipulation dismissing the action with prejudice. The stipulation stated that the claims were settled for $25,000 plus $400 in costs, for a total of $25,400.

What was the dispute about?

According to the Second Circuit’s summary of the allegations, Piers Gardner, an English barrister, claimed that Surie Lefkowitz retained him in May 2002 to provide legal services to prevent the extradition of her brother from Austria to the United States, and that, after an initial payment in 2002, she breached an agreement to pay his fees. Gardner filed a diversity action in the Southern District of New York in June 2005. In October 2005, the district court entered a default judgment against Lefkowitz in the amount of $180,493.82.

After prolonged proceedings, including a previous appeal to the Second Circuit, the district court issued an order on March 30, 2017, denying Lefkowitz’s motion to vacate the default judgment and for sanctions. She appealed from that order.

What did the Second Circuit hold?

In a September 21, 2018 summary order, a panel of Judges Jon O. Newman, Dennis Jacobs, and Rosemary S. Pooler vacated the district court’s judgment insofar as it had denied the motion to vacate the default judgment, and remanded for further proceedings.

On service of process, Lefkowitz argued that so-called “nail and mail” service under CPLR 308(4) was invalid because Gardner had not exercised due diligence in attempting personal delivery or delivery to a person of suitable age and discretion. The court of appeals called it “a close question,” but held that service was acceptable. The process server had made three attempts at personal service, on June 17, 2005, at 10:20 a.m.; July 5, 2005, at 7:00 a.m.; and July 6, 2005, at 6:55 p.m., and had spoken with a neighbor who confirmed that Lefkowitz lived at the address. Those efforts, the court said, were sufficient to permit nail-and-mail service.

On whether Lefkowitz had shown a meritorious defense, the court of appeals rejected her argument that any agreement was unenforceable because Gardner had not produced a written retainer or letter of engagement. The New York provision she relied on, 22 N.Y.C.R.R. § 1215.2(d), does not apply to a representation in which the attorney is admitted to practice in another jurisdiction and maintains no office in New York, or in which no material portion of the services are to be rendered in New York. The court held that she had not presented facts supporting the applicability of that provision where Gardner, an English barrister, provided legal services in Switzerland to contest an extradition from Austria.

The court also discussed an alleged June 2002 partial payment of Gardner’s fees by wire transfer from a Brooklyn law firm. Lefkowitz, the court said, did not contest the fact of that partial payment or that she made it; she asserted instead that the law firm did not serve as her lawyers. Assuming that New York law governed, the court of appeals held that the undisputed partial payment established an agreement sufficient to support Gardner’s claims, citing the rule that an agreement supporting an account-stated claim may be implied if the debtor makes partial payment.

The court nonetheless found “a substantial question” as to whether Lefkowitz had a meritorious defense regarding the scope of any retention. Her brother was purportedly extradited to the United States on June 9, 2002, but the vast majority of the fees Gardner claimed were incurred after that date. Gardner claimed that Lefkowitz had agreed he would perform post-extradition work involving the United Nations Human Rights Committee. The court said her denial of an agreement with Gardner implicitly raised a question of fact about whether the scope of any agreement encompassed the services that resulted in those later fees. Further proceedings were therefore required to determine the scope of the agreement. The court did not consider Lefkowitz’s remaining arguments at that time.

What did the January 23, 2019 order do?

On remand, Judge Batts signed a short order docketed as Document 93. The official docket text states that the court granted the motion to vacate, ECF No. 80, and denied a January 22, 2019 letter motion for an extension of time to file a reply, ECF No. 92, because the reply had been due on January 15, 2019. The order then states: “In light of the Second Circuit’s ruling, the Default Judgment is VACATED.” The defendant was directed to move or answer the complaint within 30 days of the order, and the court said there would be no extensions.

What happened after the default was vacated?

On May 1, 2019, Judge Batts so-ordered a stipulation docketed as Document 97. The official docket text states that the parties stipulated to dismiss and discontinue the action with prejudice under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 41(a), and that the claims which were the subject of the action, or otherwise arose out of the incidents alleged in the complaint, were settled for $25,000 plus $400 in costs, for a total of $25,400, in full satisfaction of all claims for damages, costs, disbursements, and legal fees.

What did the decision not decide?

  • The January 23, 2019 order is a short order vacating the default judgment in light of the Second Circuit’s ruling. It does not contain an independent written analysis of service, the merits of the fee claim, or the scope of any retainer.
  • The Second Circuit did not hold that there was no agreement at all. It held that an undisputed partial payment could support an agreement sufficient to support Gardner’s claims, and it remanded for further proceedings on the scope of that agreement.
  • The court of appeals did not reach Lefkowitz’s remaining arguments, including her argument about prejudice from vacating the default.
  • The Second Circuit decision is a summary order. The order itself states that rulings by summary order do not have precedential effect.
  • Vacating the default judgment was not a determination of how much, if anything, was ultimately owed. The later dismissal was by a so-ordered stipulation, not a trial verdict or a judicial determination of the fee dispute.
