Commercial notices to cure and Yellowstone injunctions in Queens

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

A New York commercial tenant that receives a notice to cure, notice of default, or threat of lease termination can ask the Supreme Court for a Yellowstone injunction, which tolls (pauses) the lease’s cure period while the court decides whether there is a default, so the tenant can still cure if it loses. The tenant generally must show that it holds a commercial lease, received a notice of default, notice to cure, or threat of termination, requested relief before the lease was terminated, and is prepared and able to cure by any means short of vacating (Graubard Mollen Horowitz Pomeranz & Shapiro v 600 Third Ave. Assocs., 93 NY2d 508 [1999]). Timing is the critical issue. The doctrine takes its name from a 1968 Court of Appeals case about a supermarket in Forest Hills, Queens, where the tenant lost its lease because it did not obtain a stay before the cure period ran.

What is a notice to cure?

Many commercial leases let the landlord serve a written notice identifying a claimed default (for example, unpaid additional rent, an insurance or repair obligation, an unapproved alteration, or a use issue) and giving the tenant a set number of days to cure. If the tenant does not cure within that period, the lease may allow the landlord to serve a notice terminating the lease and then bring a holdover proceeding. The exact steps, deadlines, and service rules come from the lease, so the lease and every notice should be read together on the day the notice arrives.

What happened in the original Yellowstone case, and why does timing matter?

In First National Stores v Yellowstone Shopping Center, 21 NY2d 630 (1968), the tenant leased a supermarket building in the Yellowstone Shopping Center in Forest Hills. After a Fire Department sprinkler order, the landlord sent a 10-day notice of default, which the tenant received on February 27, 1967. The tenant started a declaratory judgment action the next day by serving a summons, but its later motion papers contained no stay. Once the 10 days ran, the landlord sent a termination notice on March 10, 1967. The Court of Appeals held that the tenant was responsible for the sprinkler system, that the lease had terminated according to its terms, and that the court was powerless to revive it absent fraud, mutual mistake, or another basis for reformation.

The practical lesson is that filing a lawsuit is not enough. The tenant needs a court order tolling the cure period before the cure period expires and before the landlord terminates the lease. Check how the lease counts the cure period (for example, from receipt or from mailing) and act well before the last day.

What must a tenant show to get a Yellowstone injunction?

The Court of Appeals in Graubard Mollen stated that the tenant must demonstrate that:

  1. it holds a commercial lease;
  2. it received from the landlord a notice of default, a notice to cure, or a threat of termination of the lease;
  3. it requested injunctive relief before the lease was terminated; and
  4. it is prepared and maintains the ability to cure the alleged default by any means short of vacating the premises.

The Court of Appeals has observed that courts granted these injunctions routinely to avoid forfeiture and accepted far less than the usual showing for a preliminary injunction (Post v 120 E. End Ave. Corp., 62 NY2d 19, 25 [1984], as quoted in 159 MP Corp. v Redbridge Bedford, LLC, 33 NY3d 353 (2019)). Even so, relief is discretionary and depends on the record.

What does a Yellowstone injunction do, and what does it not do?

  • It preserves the status quo. Its limited purpose is to stop the running of the cure period while the court decides whether there is a default, so a tenant that loses on the merits can still cure and keep the lease.
  • It does not rewrite the lease. In Graubard Mollen, the tenant had to pay the rent arrears and the interest on late rent provided in the lease after it lost on the merits. The Court explained that the injunction “protected the firm from eviction; it did not rewrite the lease.”
  • Conditions are common. In that case the court conditioned the injunction on escrow deposits equal to the rent. The Court of Appeals described such conditions as protection for landlords, much like a bond, that do not change the parties’ underlying rights.
  • It does not excuse lease conditions. The Court also noted that a Yellowstone injunction cannot relieve a tenant of conditions precedent in the lease, such as conditions to renewal.

Why is a Yellowstone injunction filed in Supreme Court instead of Civil Court?

The Court of Appeals has explained that requests for Yellowstone relief “are necessarily made in Supreme Court rather than Civil Court, which lacks authority to issue injunctive relief” (159 MP Corp.). The request is usually made by order to show cause in a declaratory judgment action about the claimed default. For Queens premises, that generally means the Supreme Court, Queens County, at 88-11 Sutphin Boulevard, Jamaica. A landlord’s holdover or nonpayment proceeding, by contrast, is a summary proceeding typically brought in Civil Court.

Can a lease waive the right to seek a Yellowstone injunction?

In 159 MP Corp. (2019), the Court of Appeals enforced a commercial lease clause in which the tenants waived the right to bring a declaratory judgment action. Because there was no pending action, there was no basis for Yellowstone relief. New York then enacted Real Property Law § 235-h, which provides that no commercial lease shall contain a provision waiving or prohibiting a tenant’s right to bring a declaratory judgment action about any lease provision, term, or condition, and that any such waiver is null and void as against public policy. If your lease contains a waiver like this, particularly an older lease, have counsel review how § 235-h applies to it.

Does Yellowstone relief apply to residential tenants?

No. A commercial lease is one of the four requirements. Residential tenants have different, statutory protections.

What should a commercial landlord consider?

  • Draft the notice to cure to track the lease exactly: the specific default, the cure period, and the required method of service.
  • Where the tenant seeks Yellowstone relief, ask the court for appropriate conditions, such as continued payment of rent or an escrow or undertaking.
  • Lease remedies such as interest on late rent survive the injunction (Graubard Mollen).
  • Consider whether the tenant can actually cure by means short of vacating; that is one of the four requirements.

What documents should I gather when a notice to cure arrives?

  • The lease, every rider, amendment, and guaranty
  • The notice to cure or notice of default, the envelope or proof of service, and the date it was actually received
  • A calendar of the cure period as the lease defines it, and the date of any termination notice
  • Records relevant to the claimed default (ledger, insurance certificates, permits, alteration approvals, violation notices)
  • Proof of the tenant’s ability to cure (funds, contractors, insurance quotes)
  • Correspondence with the landlord, the managing agent, and any lender
