Queens eviction cases: how residential and commercial evictions work in New York

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In Queens, a landlord generally recovers possession through a summary proceeding under Article 7 of the New York Real Property Actions and Proceedings Law (RPAPL), heard in the Civil Court of the City of New York, Queens County, at 89-17 Sutphin Boulevard in Jamaica. A tenant or lawful occupant of a dwelling may not be removed except in such a proceeding, and after a judgment the warrant is carried out by a sheriff or city marshal who must first give at least 14 days’ written notice. Which notices, defenses, and timelines apply depends on whether the case is nonpayment or holdover, whether the space is residential or commercial, and whether the apartment is rent regulated or covered by New York’s Good Cause Eviction Law.

Which court hears Queens eviction cases?

Residential and commercial summary proceedings for Queens property are generally brought in the Civil Court of the City of New York, Queens County, at 89-17 Sutphin Boulevard, Jamaica, NY 11435. Residential cases are heard in the Housing Court; the court’s directory lists the Landlord-Tenant Clerk’s Office at (718) 262-7145. Check the official NYC Civil Court directory for current hours and contacts.

Civil Court cannot issue injunctions, so a commercial tenant seeking a Yellowstone injunction must go to the Supreme Court, Queens County, at 88-11 Sutphin Boulevard (see notices to cure and Yellowstone injunctions).

What notice must a residential landlord give before not renewing a lease or raising rent 5% or more?

Real Property Law § 226-c requires written notice when a landlord does not intend to renew a residential tenancy, or intends to renew with a rent increase of 5% or more. The notice period is based on the longer of the tenant’s total occupancy or the lease term:

  • Less than one year of occupancy and no lease of at least one year: at least 30 days.
  • One to two years, or a lease of at least one but less than two years: at least 60 days.
  • More than two years, or a lease of at least two years: at least 90 days.

If timely notice is not given, the tenancy continues under its existing terms from the date actual written notice was given until the notice period runs. The statute also requires the notice to include the § 231-c Good Cause statement, and contains an exception for certain cooperative corporations and their shareholder-tenants.

Does the Good Cause Eviction Law apply in Queens?

Yes, for covered apartments. Article 6-A of the Real Property Law, the Good Cause Eviction Law, applies in New York City (RPL § 212). It covers housing accommodations unless an exemption in RPL § 214 applies. Exemptions include, among others:

  • premises owned by a “small landlord” (a landlord claiming this exemption in an eviction case must disclose the natural-person owners and their other units);
  • owner-occupied buildings with no more than ten units;
  • units already subject to rent regulation, or required to be affordable under a regulatory agreement or similar restriction;
  • condominium and cooperative units, and units in buildings under an offering plan;
  • buildings with a certificate of occupancy issued on or after January 1, 2009, for 30 years after issuance; and
  • units with rent above 245% of the HUD fair market rent for the county, as published by the NYS Division of Housing and Community Renewal.

For a covered apartment, a court may remove the tenant only on a ground listed in RPL § 216, even if the lease has expired. The grounds include unpaid rent (unless it resulted from an unreasonable increase, which is presumed when the increase exceeds the local rent standard), violation of a substantial lease obligation not cured within ten days of written notice, nuisance or substantial damage, illegal use, unreasonable refusal of access, the landlord’s or a qualifying family member’s good-faith personal use (proven by clear and convincing evidence and not available against tenants 65 or older or disabled tenants), good-faith demolition or withdrawal from the rental market, and refusal of reasonable lease changes noticed 30 to 90 days before expiration.

Rent-stabilized and rent-controlled apartments are governed by separate rent-regulation laws that this page does not summarize. Article 6-A is currently scheduled to be repealed on June 15, 2034. Whether a particular unit is covered or exempt is fact-specific and often disputed.

What happens after a judgment?

  • Warrant and notice. The warrant is directed to the sheriff or a city marshal, who must give at least 14 days’ written notice and may execute only on a business day between sunrise and sunset (RPAPL § 749(1)–(2)).
  • Court’s power to stay or vacate. The court may stay or vacate a warrant for good cause before execution, or restore a tenant to possession afterward (RPAPL § 749(3)).
  • Residential stays. For dwelling premises, the court may stay a warrant for up to one year on the occupant’s application if the statutory hardship conditions are met, usually conditioned on deposits for use and occupancy; this stay is not available against an occupant the landlord proves is objectionable (RPAPL § 753(1)–(3)).
  • Cure after a breach-of-lease judgment. Where a residential case is based on breach of a lease provision, the court must grant a 30-day stay of the warrant so the respondent can correct the breach (RPAPL § 753(4)). Lease clauses waiving § 753 are void (§ 753(5)).

How long does a Queens eviction take?

No one can promise a timeline. Some nonpayment proceedings are governed by RPAPL § 732 where court rules adopt it: the notice of petition is returnable before the clerk within ten days of service, a respondent who does not answer within that period risks a default judgment, and the notice of petition must explain those requirements. Holdover cases, cases with contested defenses, and cases involving regulated or Good Cause units often take longer. Read every court paper you receive the day it arrives and calendar each date.

How are commercial evictions different?

  • The lease controls much more: notice and cure periods, conditional limitations, and how notices must be served.
  • The residential protections above (Good Cause, RPL § 226-c, and RPAPL § 753 stays) apply to residential premises, not stores or offices.
  • The 14-day written rent demand in RPAPL § 711(2) applies to rent defaults generally; check the lease for any additional notice requirements.
  • A commercial tenant served with a notice to cure may need to seek a Yellowstone injunction before the cure period ends.
  • Lease drafting matters. Our case note on Lee v Chin (App Div 1st Dept 2026) discusses how a merger clause and reviewed riders defeated claims about a lease end date.

What documents should I gather first?

  • The lease, every rider and amendment, and any renewal or non-renewal notices
  • Every predicate notice served (rent demand, notice to cure, termination, non-renewal), with proof of when and how it was served
  • The rent ledger, receipts, bank records, and any rental-assistance correspondence
  • The notice of petition, petition, and any prior stipulations or orders
  • Records bearing on rent regulation or Good Cause coverage (building size, owner-occupancy, certificate of occupancy date, rent history)
  • Emails, texts, and letters about rent, repairs, access, or the alleged violation

FAQ

How long does a Queens eviction take?

There is no fixed timeline. Timing depends on the type of case, whether the predicate notices and service were proper, the court’s calendar, whether the respondent answers and raises defenses, motion practice, and whether the parties resolve the case by stipulation. After a judgment, the marshal or sheriff must still give at least 14 days’ written notice before executing a warrant (RPAPL § 749), and residential occupants may ask for a stay under RPAPL § 753.

Holdover vs. nonpayment: what’s the difference?

A nonpayment proceeding (RPAPL § 711(2)) seeks possession because rent is unpaid, after a written rent demand giving at least 14 days’ notice. A holdover proceeding (RPAPL § 711(1)) seeks possession because the tenant stayed after the term expired or was terminated, which usually turns on the lease and any required termination or non-renewal notices.

What documents should I gather first?

The lease and every rider or amendment, all notices served (with proof of how and when they were served), the rent ledger and proof of payments, any prior stipulations or court papers, and key emails and texts. For residential units, also gather anything showing rent-regulation status or Good Cause Eviction Law coverage or exemption.

Can the case settle without trial?

Yes. Many cases resolve through a written stipulation that sets a payment plan, a move-out date, repairs, or other conditions. A stipulation is enforceable, so its deadlines and default terms should be read carefully before signing.

Do commercial and residential evictions follow the same rules?

No. Both use RPAPL Article 7 summary proceedings, but many protections are limited to residential premises, including the Good Cause Eviction Law, the non-renewal notice periods in Real Property Law § 226-c, and the hardship and cure stays in RPAPL § 753. Commercial cases are driven largely by the lease, and a commercial tenant facing a notice to cure may need to seek a Yellowstone injunction in Supreme Court.

Does a defective notice matter?

It can. Predicate notices such as the 14-day rent demand, a non-renewal notice, or a notice to cure are often required before a case can proceed, and defects in content or service can be significant. The effect depends on the type of case, the governing statute or lease, and how the issue is raised.
