Courchevel 1850 LLC v Stern (E.D.N.Y. 2018): RPAPL 1304 notice after the borrower moves

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In Courchevel 1850 LLC v. Stern, No. 17-CV-1794 (NGG) (JO), a memorandum and order of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York filed June 28, 2018, Judge Nicholas G. Garaufis granted defendant Isaac Stern’s motion to dismiss a mortgage-foreclosure complaint and dismissed the complaint without prejudice. The court held that RPAPL § 1304 requires pre-foreclosure notice to the borrower of what is at origination a “home loan,” even if the borrower later moves out of the property securing the loan. The plaintiff’s allegation that no such notice was required because Stern no longer owned or resided at the property was not enough, at the pleading stage, to state a foreclosure claim under New York law.

What was the dispute about?

The plaintiff sued to foreclose a mortgage on real property at 355A Halsey Street in Brooklyn. According to the complaint, which the court accepted as true for purposes of the motion, Stern borrowed $190,000 from National City Bank in 2005 under a line of credit secured by that mortgage. After later assignments, the note was held by the plaintiff. The complaint alleged that Stern remained in default. On December 15, 2016, the plaintiff notified Stern that it intended to treat the outstanding balance as immediately due and gave him 30 days to cure, as the mortgage required. The plaintiff did not send an RPAPL § 1304 pre-foreclosure notice, because Stern no longer owned or resided at the property and, in the plaintiff’s view, was not entitled to that notice. The parties appeared to agree that, after Stern executed the note and mortgage but before this action was filed, he sold the property, which was subdivided into four units belonging to new owners. The plaintiff commenced the action on March 30, 2017, naming Stern, the new owners, and other alleged junior lienholders. Stern moved to dismiss on the ground that the plaintiff had not served him with RPAPL § 1304 notice.

What legal question did the court decide?

The court treated the motion as turning on a discrete question of law. Under New York law, proper service of RPAPL § 1304 notice on the borrower is a condition precedent to commencing a foreclosure action, and the plaintiff has the burden of establishing that condition. The question, as the court framed it, was whether a plaintiff carries that burden at the pleading stage by alleging that it was not required to serve RPAPL § 1304 notice because the borrower no longer owned or resided at the subject property. The court answered that question in the negative.

What did the court hold about RPAPL 1304?

In the court’s view, RPAPL § 1304 requires that pre-foreclosure notice be sent to the borrower of what is at origination a “home loan,” even if the borrower later moves out of the property securing the loan. The court noted that the New York Court of Appeals had not answered that question, and that it was predicting how that court would resolve the statute. It also noted that several other decisions in the Eastern District of New York appeared to have adopted the plaintiff’s contrary reading.

Reading the statute as a whole, the court relied in particular on two provisions then in effect. First, RPAPL § 1304(3) provided that the 90-day waiting period after the notice would not apply, or would cease to apply, if the borrower no longer occupied the residence as the borrower’s principal dwelling, but also said that nothing in that subsection relieved the lender, assignee, or mortgage loan servicer of the obligation to send the notice, which remained a condition precedent to commencing a foreclosure proceeding. Second, RPAPL § 1304(2) required the notice to be sent to the borrower by registered or certified mail and also by first-class mail to the borrower’s last known address and to the residence that is the subject of the mortgage. The court reasoned that much of that language would be superfluous if notice were required only when the borrower still lived at the mortgaged property. The court also pointed to contemporaneous CPLR 3408 language requiring a mandatory settlement conference in a residential foreclosure involving a “home loan” as defined in RPAPL § 1304 “in which the defendant is a resident of the property subject to foreclosure,” language the court said would likewise be surplusage under the plaintiff’s reading. A footnote observed that courts in the district have held that Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 16 displaces CPLR 3408 in federal foreclosure actions; the court used that statutory text only as an aid in interpreting RPAPL § 1304.

The court acknowledged that this interpretation “fits somewhat awkwardly with the likely purpose of the statute,” which was directed at aiding homeowners facing foreclosure, and that it seemed somewhat unlikely the Legislature anticipated helping borrowers who had not lived in the mortgaged property for years. It concluded, however, that the legislative history was not clear on the point and that there was no need to consult legislative history where the meaning of the statute itself was clear.

Accordingly, the court held that the plaintiff’s allegation that it did not need to serve notice on Stern before starting foreclosure proceedings was insufficient to state a foreclosure claim under New York law. The motion to dismiss was granted, and the complaint was dismissed without prejudice.

What did the decision not decide?

  • The ruling came on a motion to dismiss for failure to state a claim. The court accepted the complaint’s well-pleaded factual allegations as true for that purpose. It is not a trial verdict about the default, the amount due, or who later owned the subdivided units.
  • The dismissal was without prejudice. The order did not enter a judgment on the merits with prejudice.
  • The decision is a federal district court’s interpretation of New York law as of 2018. The court itself noted that the New York Court of Appeals had not decided the question and that other judges in the same district had read the statute differently.
  • The court did not apply CPLR 3408 in this federal action. It discussed that statute only as context for interpreting RPAPL § 1304.
  • The court noted later amendments to RPAPL § 1304 but did not treat those amendments as changing the question before it.
