1211 Gilbert Ave. Holdings LLC v Berri (Sup Ct, Bronx County 2025): a time-of-the-essence closing, the down payment, and a cancelled notice of pendency

Written and reviewed by Jason Berke, Esq., Law Offices of Jason Berke PC, Kew Gardens, Queens · Last reviewed November 7, 2026

In 1211 Gilbert Ave. Holdings LLC v Berri, 2025 NY Slip Op 50142(U) [85 Misc 3d 1212(A)], decided February 6, 2025, Supreme Court, Bronx County (Hummel, J.) granted the seller’s pre-answer motion to dismiss the buyer’s complaint under CPLR 3211(a)(1) and (a)(7). The court held that the seller set a proper time-of-the-essence closing date, that the buyer was not ready, willing, and able to close on that date, and that the buyer therefore could not obtain specific performance or contract damages. The court allowed the seller to retain the $35,000 deposit and the interest that had accumulated on it in escrow, cancelled a notice of pendency dated October 24, 2023, and denied the buyer’s cross-motion. The slip opinion states that it is uncorrected and will not be published in the printed Official Reports.

What was the dispute about?

The buyer sued for specific performance and damages after a proposed cash sale of 1211 Gilbert Place, Bronx, NY 10474. The July 7, 2023 contract fixed a $700,000 purchase price and a $35,000 down payment, with no financing contingency. Closing was to take place on or about 45 days after the contract date (which the court treated as expiring on August 21, 2023), or on reasonable notice by the purchaser.

As described in the decision, the seller later sought to schedule a closing, the buyer repeatedly deferred setting a date, and a September 15, 2023 date was discussed. On September 12, 2023, the buyer said it could not close that week and requested September 28. The next day the seller served a time-of-the-essence letter setting closing for September 28, 2023, at 1:00 p.m. The buyer later objected that 15 days’ notice was unreasonable because of weekends and Jewish holidays. The court noted that the buyer had proposed the September 28 date, that most of the cited holidays fell over a weekend, and that a later reference to “Passover” would not have occurred until April 2024. The buyer did not appear at the September 28 closing. The seller’s transactional attorney attested that the seller was ready, willing, and able to close that day.

After the missed closing, the buyer also argued that the seller could not deliver clear title, pointing to an undated title-company email about a quitclaim deed, no-consideration and creditors’ rights affidavits, and returns versus Carmen Rijo, plus a follow-up email dated October 3, 2023.

What did the court decide about the time-of-the-essence date?

The court held that the seller set a proper time-of-the-essence closing date, selected by the buyer, by sending clear, unequivocal notice and a reasonable time to close. It found the time reasonable in light of the buyer’s request for that date, the seller’s expressed commitment to close in September 2023, the cited holidays, the absence of a financing contingency, the parties’ business sophistication, the absence of any claim of prejudice, the buyer’s prior delays, and the buyer’s failure to give a reasonable justification for not closing on the all-cash deal.

What did the court decide about specific performance and breach of contract?

A buyer seeking to hold a seller in breach, the court wrote, must show it was ready, willing, and able to perform on the time-of-the-essence date. The court found the seller established that the buyer was not, including through emails showing the buyer would not appear, and that the buyer submitted no proof of its financial ability to pay the $700,000 cash price.

The court also rejected the argument that a title-insurability issue excused the buyer. That issue arose after the missed closing. Even assuming the seller could not deliver insurable title, the court held that would not relieve the buyer of the burden to show it was ready, willing, and able to perform.

The court further held that paragraph 23 of the first rider, with its “sole obligation” language, limited the buyer’s remedy if the seller could not transfer the premises to return of the down payment. The seller’s argument that the contract foreclosed specific performance was unanswered in opposition and was treated as conceded. The court therefore dismissed both the specific-performance claim and the breach-of-contract claim for damages.

What did the court decide about the deposit and the notice of pendency?

Because the buyer defaulted without a lawful excuse, and because paragraph 23(a) allowed the seller to retain the down payment if the purchaser defaulted by failing to close, the court granted the seller’s request to retain the $35,000 deposit. That deposit was 5% of the $700,000 price. The court granted the request for interest “to the extent that Defendant shall retain the Deposit and the interest accumulated thereon in the escrow account.”

The notice of pendency dated October 24, 2023, covering Block 2762, Lot 64 (1211 Gilbert Place, Bronx), was cancelled because the only claim affecting title—specific performance—had been dismissed.

A request first raised on reply for leave to move under CPLR 6514(c) for damages based on an alleged bad-faith filing of the notice of pendency was denied as improperly raised for the first time on reply. The buyer’s cross-motion for nunc pro tunc service or a preliminary injunction was denied as moot.

What did the decision not decide?

  • It is a decision on a pre-answer motion to dismiss based on the complaint and the documents and affidavits submitted. It is not a trial verdict.
  • The slip opinion will not be published in the printed Official Reports.
  • The court did not decide a CPLR 6514(c) claim for extra damages based on the notice of pendency; it denied that request because it was first raised on reply.
  • The reasonableness of a time-of-the-essence date depends on the facts. A different notice period, a financed deal, or different delay history can come out differently.
  • The title emails are discussed only as they relate to this missed closing. The decision does not announce a general rule about every title objection.

What should parties check before a time-of-the-essence closing?

Time-of-the-essence closing checklist
CheckWhy it matters
Confirm in writing who proposed the closing date before objecting that the notice period is too short.In this case the court relied on the buyer having requested the September 28, 2023 date.
Treat a time-of-the-essence letter as needing clear, unequivocal notice and a reasonable time to perform, judged against the parties’ prior delays, sophistication, financing contingencies, and any claimed prejudice.Those were the factors the court used when it found the notice here reasonable.
Be prepared to prove you were ready, willing, and able to close on that date, including proof of funds on an all-cash deal.The court found no proof of the buyer’s financial ability to pay the $700,000 cash price.
Raise title objections before the closing you fail to attend. An issue that appears after a missed date may not excuse the failure to appear.The title-insurability issue in this case arose after the missed closing.
Read the default and “sole obligation” or liquidated-damages clauses. They can limit both the seller’s remedy (often the deposit) and the buyer’s remedy (often return of the deposit).Paragraphs 23 and 23(a) of this contract limited the remedies, and the court allowed the seller to keep the deposit and escrow interest.
Remember that a notice of pendency usually falls if the claim affecting title is dismissed.The notice of pendency here was cancelled after the specific-performance claim was dismissed.
